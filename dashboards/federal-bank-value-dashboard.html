The Business, in Plain Terms

Federal Bank is a Kerala-headquartered private-sector bank with 757 branches across 17 states and union territories as of March 2026, plus representative offices in Abu Dhabi and Dubai and an IFSC Banking Unit in GIFT City. It has historically been the preferred bank of India's Non-Resident Indian (NRI) diaspora, and this NRI deposit franchise remains a structural low-cost funding advantage over most private-bank peers.

The Bank operates across four broad verticals — Retail Banking, Business Banking, Commercial Banking, and Corporate & Institutional Banking — supplemented by two subsidiaries (FedServ for back-office operations; Fedbank Financial Services, a gold-loan and secured-lending NBFC that IPO'd in FY24) and a 30% stake in Ageas Federal Life Insurance, a bancassurance joint venture.

Why this matters for a value investor: A bank's moat is its deposit franchise, not its loan book. Federal Bank's NRI-heavy, retail-dominated deposit base (CASA + retail term deposits) gives it a durable, relatively rate-insensitive funding cost advantage — the single most important structural strength to track across the tables below.

Cash Flow (Standalone, ₹ Cr)

Federal Bank does not disclose a conventional "free cash flow" figure in the way a manufacturer would — for a bank, growth in the loan book itself consumes operating cash (an "Increase in Advances" line item), so a bank in growth mode structurally shows working-capital-driven swings in operating cash flow that are not comparable to a non-financial company.

Cash Flow by Activity: FY 2024-25 vs FY 2025-26

Why the pattern looks this way: Operating cash flow fell from ₹10,073 Cr to ₹7,182 Cr in FY26 mainly because advances grew faster (₹31,494 Cr net increase vs ₹26,238 Cr in FY25) while deposit growth was roughly flat in absolute terms — a bank funding faster loan growth naturally shows lower net operating cash. Investing outflow widened sharply (–₹11,055 Cr vs –₹3,744 Cr) due to a large step-up in purchases of Held-to-Maturity investments, and financing activities swung negative as the Bank repaid net borrowings and paid dividends, partly offset by ₹1,549 Cr raised via share warrants. None of this signals distress — it is the normal cash signature of a bank simultaneously growing its book and building its investment portfolio.

Schedule V

Pros & Cons

+ Decadal-best asset quality

Net NPA fell from 0.96% (FY22) to just 0.20% (FY26), with Provision Coverage Ratio rising to 87.07% — among the cleanest loan books in Indian private banking, reducing the risk of a future credit-cost shock eating into earnings.

+ Strengthening capital position

CRAR improved from 15.77% to 17.25% over the period, well above the 11.50% regulatory minimum, giving the Bank ample headroom to keep growing advances at 15%+ without needing dilutive capital raises soon.

+ Durable NRI deposit franchise

NR deposits crossed ₹1,02,620 Cr in FY26 — a structurally sticky, geographically diversified funding source most peers cannot easily replicate, supporting a lower and more stable cost of deposits over the cycle.

+ Improving operating efficiency

Cost-to-Income Ratio improved from 54.50% (FY24) to 52.27% (FY26) even while the Bank added 148 branches in FY26 alone — suggesting digital investment is starting to pay off in productivity, not just customer experience.

+ Diversified fee-generating subsidiaries

Fedbank Financial Services (gold loans/secured lending, 60.79% owned) grew its net profit 53% YoY to ₹343.6 Cr in FY26, and Ageas Federal Life Insurance delivered its 14th consecutive profitable year — both add optionality beyond core banking.

− Return on Equity has been declining

ROE peaked at 15.02% in FY23 and has fallen every year since, to 11.47% in FY26, even as PAT kept growing — net worth has been compounding faster than profit, meaning the Bank isn't yet deploying its expanding capital base as efficiently as it once did.

− CASA ratio has been volatile and structurally lower than peers

CASA ratio fell from 36.94% (FY22) to a low of 29.38% (FY24) before recovering to 32.94% (FY26) — still below several large private-bank peers, meaning a larger share of funding comes from costlier term deposits.

− Profit growth has nearly stalled the last two years

PAT grew just 8.9% in FY25 and only 1.6% in FY26, a sharp deceleration from the 30%+ growth seen in FY23 — investors extrapolating the 5-year CAGR forward should recognise the recent trend is much slower.

− Rising operating expenses from expansion

Operating expenses grew to ₹7,892 Cr in FY26 from ₹7,168 Cr, driven by technology, branch expansion and people costs — a necessary investment, but one that needs to keep translating into proportionate income growth to avoid margin erosion.

− Credit cost has started rising off a very low base

Credit cost rose to 73 bps in FY26 from 38 bps in FY25 — still low in absolute terms, but the direction is worth watching given the Bank has been growing higher-yielding (and typically higher-risk) segments like gold loans, credit cards and CV/CE financing faster than its overall book.

Schedule VI

Valuation — What Price Makes Sense

For a bank, Price-to-Book (P/B) is the primary valuation lens rather than P/E, because a bank's core asset (its loan book) is already carried close to fair value on the balance sheet, and P/B better captures the return the Bank is earning on shareholders' capital (its ROE) relative to what you're paying for that capital.

At a recent price of ₹352 and FY26 book value per share of ₹157.00, Federal Bank trades at roughly 2.24x trailing book value. Over the FY22–FY26 period, the Bank's book value has compounded at 15.3% while ROE has been on a declining trend (15.0% → 11.5%) — a P/B multiple should be judged against where ROE is heading, not just where it has been.

The arithmetic: Federal Bank's own 5-year P/B range (using FY-end book value and roughly contemporaneous share prices) has moved from well under 1.5x in FY22–FY23 to over 2x more recently, as the market re-rated the stock on cleaner asset quality and a change in CEO/strategic outlook. At ~2.24x book with an 11.47% ROE, the implied earnings yield on book value is modest relative to the multiple being paid — the market is pricing in either a further ROE recovery (driven by the Bank's stated cost-to-income and NIM improvement targets) or continued re-rating toward larger private-bank peers, rather than today's ROE alone. A disciplined value investor would want to see ROE trend back up toward 14–15% before paying the top of this range, and would look for a pullback toward the 1.4–1.8x P/B band (roughly ₹220–₹280 at current book value) as a more comfortable entry zone, with the deep-value zone below 1.4x historically only available during broad market drawdowns.

Glossary

PAT (Profit After Tax)
The bank's bottom-line net profit after all expenses, provisions and taxes — the standard measure of annual earnings.
NII (Net Interest Income)
Interest earned on loans/investments minus interest paid on deposits/borrowings — a bank's core "spread" income before fees.
NIM (Net Interest Margin)
NII divided by average earning assets, expressed as a percentage — measures how profitably the Bank is lending relative to its asset base.
Gross NPA / Net NPA
Loans where repayment is overdue beyond regulatory thresholds ("Non-Performing Assets"), before (Gross) and after (Net) providing for expected losses — the key asset-quality red-flag indicator.
Provision Coverage Ratio (PCR)
The percentage of bad loans already provisioned for; a higher PCR means fewer future profit shocks if those loans are eventually written off.
CASA Ratio
Current Account + Savings Account deposits as a percentage of total deposits — these are the cheapest, stickiest source of bank funding, so a higher CASA ratio generally means a lower cost of funds.
CRAR (Capital to Risk-weighted Assets Ratio)
Also called Capital Adequacy Ratio; measures how much capital a bank holds against its risk-weighted loan book, as mandated by RBI/Basel III rules — a safety buffer against future losses.
Cost-to-Income Ratio
Operating expenses as a percentage of net total income — lower is more efficient; rising branch/technology investment can push this up temporarily.
ROA (Return on Assets)
PAT divided by average total assets — measures how efficiently the Bank converts its balance sheet into profit, independent of leverage.
ROE (Return on Equity)
PAT divided by average shareholders' equity — measures the return generated on shareholders' own capital, the key driver of a bank's fair P/B multiple.
EPS (Earnings Per Share)
PAT divided by weighted-average shares outstanding — the per-share slice of annual profit.
Book Value Per Share (BVPS)
Net worth (shareholders' equity) divided by shares outstanding — the accounting "net asset value" behind each share, and the base against which P/B is calculated.
P/B (Price-to-Book)
Share price divided by book value per share — the primary valuation yardstick for banks, since it captures how much the market pays for each rupee of the Bank's net assets and expected future ROE.
Credit Cost
Provisions for bad loans expressed in basis points of advances — a forward-looking measure of how much of the loan book's yield is being "given back" to cover expected losses.
CET-1 / Tier-1 / Tier-2 Capital
Regulatory capital tiers of decreasing quality/loss-absorbing capacity that together make up CRAR — CET-1 (pure equity) is the highest-quality buffer.
AUM (Assets Under Management)
Total value of loans/investments managed by a subsidiary (used here for Fedbank Financial Services' gold-loan book) — a scale metric for NBFC subsidiaries.
